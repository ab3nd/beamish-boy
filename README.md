# beamish-boy

When I was starting college in the late 90s-early 2000s, there was a style of minimalist robotics called BEAM robotics. BEAM is an acronym, but I'm not going to say for what, because literally every hobbyist page about it started by explaining that. 

If I had to explain BEAM in a single sentence, it's about building well-desgined robot bodies and controlling them with systems of coupled oscillators in order to let them operate in the real world. I get the impression that, at the time, BEAM robotics was hyped as an approach to robotics that would approach "AI" by starting at the level of a bug and working up, rather than e.g. trying to duplicate what it feels like to have full human-scale consciousness, but in LISP. Where people claim that BEAM ended up running into a dead end is that it didn't scale up. Yes, you could build a walking robot with 2-4 motors and $0.75 worth of silicon, but there wasn't a way to get more complex processing out of that.

On top of that, the dynamics of the activity in BEAM walkers' Nv networks were chaotic (or at least could be driven into chaotic regimes), and arguably worked better at the "edge of chaos", which is both _really cool_ for people who like gnarly math, and _really worrying_ for people who want to make a reliable engineering dicipline out of something. There wasn't a clear path forward from a lot of hobbyists making little creeping walkers out of salvaged electronics and a handfull of stuff from RadioShack (RIP).

When I was back in college in the 2010s, there was a style of neural network architecture called "Reservoir computing". I was actually working with neurons at the time, but they were cultured from mice by biologists and I was parsing data from them and trying to control a robot arm in a closed-loop system. Reservoir Computing (RC) took recurrent neural networks, which are great with time series data but horrible to train, and just kind of... ignored training them. Instead, the neural network was set up as a reservoir, a sort of pool of connected neurons, and the connection weights were set up so that when input was fed into them, the neurons would be active for a while and then the activity would die down. A linear readout (Ridge regresssion, MLP, etc.) would be trained on the activity patterns. Training linear readouts is way more tractable. The reservior maps the time series into a hyperspace where it is linearly seperable, even if it wasn't originally, and then the readout seperates it. 

Interestingly, the optimal configuration for a reservoir in RC can be shown to have "edge of chaos" dynamics. If it's too simple, the mapping isn't sufficiently high-dimensional for the linear classfier to work. If it's properly chaotic, it doesn't have the fading dynamics that are needed to prevent the network state being conditioned on everything that was ever put into it. 

When I saw that RC worked with both spiking neurons (under the name "echo state networks") and continuious sigmoid neurons (under the name "liquid state machines"), I was reminded of the chaotic dynamics of BEAM Nv networks, and wondered if some form of Nv network could be used as the reservoir. Given that I was working with neurons at the time, I also wondered if biological neurons could do it too. However, I was a graduate student at the time, so I didn't have the research skills, time, or money to explore either of those questions myself. 

It turns out that in both cases the answer is "yes". I forget who published on the biological neurons case, but that was while I was still at college. In 2025, Ed Reitman's group at UMass Amherst published a paper demonstrating that small-world networks of BEAM Nv neuron rings can operate as a reservoir for RC. Grids don't work. The dynamics are too chaotic and so they get driven into saturation too easily. Small world networks are known in other media for robust edge-of-chaos dynamics, and if I remember correctly, are a preferred architecture for RC reservoirs in other neuronal media (like sigmoid neurons).

So it turns out that everything I've been interested in for the last 25 years or so is all related, and it turns out that I have the money (it actually doesn't take much) and the time (uh... fuck. Well, I'll figure something out) to work in this field and build some cool stuff. 

In the words of the famous pitchman: but wait, there's more! There's another model of computation, Vector Symbolic Architectures (VSA), that provides a sort of computational metaphor that overlies all of this and can glue it together. VSAs are, in an extremely broad, jazz-handsy kind of way, an algebra over an embedding. An embedding converts an input (words, images, whatever) into a fixed-length vector over some domain. I have the most experience with Fourier Holographic Reduced Representation, which is unitary vectors of complex numbers. If you think about it, a complex number in a unitary vector is an angle in the complex plane, and since it's an angle, you can think of it as a phase angle. If you have a population of spiking neurons, and a fixed timestep (or really, anything that divides time) then the time into a timestep that a neuron fires at is a phase angle, and so a FHRR vector can be thought of as a population code. Some people have theorized that gamma waves (in the brain) are the synchronizing signal that the phase angle would be based off, but I haven't read up on that in long enough that I'm a little dubious about it. 

Of course, one could collect those population codes from the activity of a reservior of neurons, possibly built using BEAM principles, and so get an embedding of what the system is experiencing on its sensors. This shares a lot of conceptual/structural similarities with a cortical column, but that's hardly a surprise, that's what Maas designed echo state networks to be. 

So far so good, but it turns out that once you have an embedding, you can do some pretty handy stuff. First, you can compare it to some other embedding (cosine similarity, hamming distance, etc.) and see if they're similar. The larger the vectors are, the less likely they are to be similar by chance, so the more likely it is that similar-looking vectors are "about" the same things. Second, you can literally reason with them. It's possible to implement logical inference with _modus tollens_ and _modus ponens_ in the VSA framework, as well as logical operators based on a parallel between boolean operators and Birkhoff and VonNeumann's calculus of quantum mechanics. Predication, analogies, etc. are all available in VSA. Finally, deep networks and neural networks in general <3 VSAs. One way to create vectors for VSAs is to train a neural network to generate them (e.g. an autoencoder and use the latent representation as your vectors).
